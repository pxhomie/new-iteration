How to increase landing page conversion — without a full redesign.

Most conversion problems live above the fold. Fix the ten seconds that decide everything before you rebuild the whole site.

March 4, 2026

Every month a business pays for a full website redesign to fix a sagging conversion rate. Six months later the numbers haven’t moved — the site is just prettier while it underperforms.

The Redesign Trap

The story usually starts with a feeling, not a metric. The site “looks outdated”, a competitor just shipped something shiny, and the conversion rate has been flat for a year. So the budget goes to a rebuild: new visual language, new sections, three months of work. The launch feels great.

Then the analytics come in. Bounce rate barely moved. Sign-ups are where they were. Because the thing that was broken — an unclear offer, a weak first screen, a form that asks for too much — was carefully redesigned, not fixed. New paint, same leak.

Almost every audit I run starts the same way: the client asks for a new website. What they actually need is a new first screen — and that’s a two-week fix, not a three-month rebuild.

What Actually Kills Conversion

Visitors decide in seconds, and they decide above the fold. In practice, low landing page conversion almost always traces back to a handful of causes: a headline that describes the company instead of the visitor’s problem, a call to action that asks for commitment before showing proof, forms with twice the fields they need, and pages that take four seconds to paint on a phone.

None of these are fixed by a new color palette. All of them are fixed by making the first screen answer three questions instantly: what is this, is it for me, and what do I do next.

High bounce rate“Outdated design”First screen doesn’t answer “what is this and why me”

Fix the First Screen First

Write the headline before you open Figma. One sentence, in the visitor’s words, about their problem — not your company. Put one primary action next to it and demote everything else. Then move proof up: a number, a client, a result, close enough to the call to action that trust and the ask land in the same glance.

Run the ten-second test on the result: show the page to someone for ten seconds, close it, and ask what the product was and what they were supposed to do. If they can’t answer both, no section below the fold will save you.

When a Full Redesign Makes Sense

Sometimes a rebuild really is the right call — the brand has outgrown the site, the product changed under it, or the codebase makes every small change expensive. But even then, the sequence matters: fix and measure the first screen inside the current site first. That data tells you what the new design has to protect, instead of gambling the whole funnel on taste.

Redesigns done in that order launch with their conversion rate already trending up. Redesigns done on a feeling launch with a party and flat charts.

A One-Evening Landing Page Audit

You can diagnose most of it in one evening. Open the page on a phone over mobile data and watch what paints first. Cover the logo and ask if you can still tell what the company sells. Count seconds until you find the primary action. Read the headline out loud to someone who’s never seen the product. Check what stands next to the call to action — proof, or decoration. The output is a prioritized fix list that costs attention, not a redesign budget.
