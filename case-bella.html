The challenge

A working prototype is not yet a product a school can adopt.

Bella is a robot that teaches programming and artificial intelligence to children aged six to twelve through play. The hardware worked; everything around it did not exist yet. It needed one identity that holds on a plastic shell and on a screen, a site that convinces a school rather than a startup investor, and software that runs in a classroom with no reliable connection — and stores nothing about the children using it.

The brand

Pixels, because that is what a child can see a computer is made of.

The identity is built from pixel blocks, soft gradients and a bright, warm palette. It is playful enough for a six-year-old and plain enough to print on a box, badge a classroom poster or sit behind interface text. The same set of parts carries the physical product and the digital one, so the robot on the desk and the app on the screen read as one thing.

The website

The page has to earn the trust of a teacher, not just the interest of a parent.

The site leads with the robot, then the learning process, then the mission — in that order, because that is the order a school asks about them. The playful tone stays, but it sits on a plain structure: short claims, real screens, and the awkward questions answered up front, including what Bella does with data, which is nothing.

The learning app

Dragging three blocks together should feel like making the robot think.

The app is where a child builds behaviour: pick an action — find an object, drive somewhere, play a sound — and chain it into something the robot performs in the room. The AI underneath is heavy; what the child sees is a short visual flow with one decision at a time, guided prompts and clear feedback. All of it works with the connection off, so a lesson never depends on the school’s Wi-Fi.

The screens

A teacher’s view and a child’s view, built from the same parts.

The lesson screen keeps the child on one task; the dashboard gives the adult the class, the progress and the next exercise without digging. Both come out of one design system, so the product can grow without forking into two visual languages.

The deliverables

A brand, a site and a learning product, shipped as one system.

  • A pixel-based identity that works on the robot,
    the packaging and the screen
  • A marketing site built for schools first,
    with the data question answered on the page
  • A learning app where AI logic becomes
    a short visual flow a child can follow
  • One design system behind the child’s lesson
    and the teacher’s dashboard
