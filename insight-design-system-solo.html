Design system for one designer: how to stay consistent without a team.

You don’t need a systems team to work like one. A lean design system pays for itself on the second project.

March 3, 2026

A design system sounds like something only product teams with a dedicated systems squad can afford. In solo practice it’s the opposite: nobody needs one more than the designer who is also the developer-liaison, the QA and the archive.

Why Solo Designers Need a Design System

Working alone, consistency is your reputation. When button paddings drift between pages and grays multiply by accident, clients can’t name the problem — they just feel the work is loose. A personal design system is how one person ships with the polish of a team.

It’s also speed. The second project on a system starts at forty percent done: tokens, components and patterns carry over, and only the brand layer changes.

My rule: if I’ve drawn the same thing twice, it becomes a component. If I’ve decided the same thing twice, it becomes a token.

Tokens Before Components

Start with decisions, not buttons. Colors, type scale, spacing steps, radii, shadows — written down as design tokens with names that describe roles, not looks: surface, ink, accent, not “green-2”. Ten minutes of naming saves hours of guessing which of six grays is the right one.

Keep the scale small on purpose. Two font families, five to seven sizes, one spacing rhythm. Constraints are the system.

The 80/20 Component Library

You don’t need two hundred components. Audit your last three projects and you’ll find the same fifteen doing all the work: buttons, inputs, cards, navigation, modals, chips, tables. Build those properly — auto layout, variants for states, sensible naming — and leave the exotic ten percent as one-offs.

Every component gets its states drawn the day it’s born: hover, focus, disabled, error, empty. Retrofitting states is where inconsistency sneaks back in.

Documentation That Fits on One Page

Solo documentation has one reader: you, six months from now. One Figma page is enough — tokens at the top, components below, and short “when to use” notes next to anything with a twin. If it doesn’t fit on a page, it won’t be read, and a system nobody reads is decoration.

Keeping the System Alive

A system decays one small exception at a time. Book a monthly half hour: merge stray styles back into tokens, promote repeated one-offs into components, delete what nothing uses. The maintenance is boring — which is exactly why it works. Boring maintenance is what consistent work looks like from the inside.
